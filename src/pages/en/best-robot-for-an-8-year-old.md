**At 8, choose a robot programmed in blocks, with at least one sensor, that an adult can assemble in one sitting.** In England this is often Year 3; in the US, 3rd grade. A child this age can usually stay with a motivating task for 20 to 40 minutes, read a short instruction on their own and explain a simple program to you or to a friend. If they are still happier with an arrow remote and reading is slow, the [7-year guide](/en/best-robot-for-a-7-year-old/) is the better fit.

This is the age of small projects rather than single sequences: follow a black line, stop before a wall, scroll a word across a matrix of LEDs. Two names will come up again and again. **Scratch** is a free visual programming language where you snap coloured blocks together, like building bricks of code, instead of typing. **mBlock** is Makeblock's software, built on Scratch, which adds the blocks needed to drive a real robot. A child who has seen Scratch at school recognises mBlock immediately.

## What changes between 6 and 8

Compared with the [6-year guide](/en/best-robot-for-a-6-year-old/), three things become realistic. Blocks are usable because the child accepts debugging: "it didn't work, I'll change the order." A kit with screws and an illustrated manual is possible if an adult is there for the first build. Role play still matters: a robot that "guards the treasure" or "delivers the parcel" turns an abstract program into something worth finishing.

## What to look for

**Software first.** The program has to run on the computer or tablet you actually own. mBlock 5 is available for Windows, macOS and tablets; check the current Makeblock system requirements before you order rather than after. Count about an hour of your own time on the first day to install mBlock and pair Bluetooth. The steps are in [install mBlock 5 on Windows](/en/install-mblock-5-windows/), and the screen layout is explained in [first steps with mBlock 5](/en/first-steps-mblock-5/). If you would rather compare the block environments, read [mBlock vs Scratch](/en/mblock-vs-scratch/).

**Sensors are what create projects.** A line follower, an ultrasonic distance sensor or programmable LEDs turn the robot into a source of challenges: "faster than last time", "complete the course without touching a wall". A robot that only drives forward and turns runs out of ideas within a few weeks.

**Community and documentation.** A widely used robot comes with YouTube tutorials, teacher worksheets and forums, so you do not have to invent every activity. On this site, [your first mBot program](/en/first-mbot-program/), [blinking the mBot LEDs](/en/mbot-activity-blink-leds/) and the [one-hour mBot project ideas](/en/mbot-one-hour-project-ideas/) show the kind of path a family can follow.

**Total budget, not sticker price.** Plan rechargeable AA batteries, sometimes a play mat, and later perhaps an extra sensor. A simpler robot you can power and extend is better than a larger kit left half-built. The [mBot accessories guide](/en/mbot-accessories-where-to-start/) lists what is worth adding and what is not.

## A simple decision

They like building and you have one evening: mBot. They want to program immediately and dislike screws: Codey Rocky. You want a small, playful build at a lower price and accept a shorter coding life: Penguin Bot.

mBot2, with its colour screen, Wi-Fi and Python, is easier to justify at 10 if the budget is there. Do not buy it at 8 just to "be ahead" while blocks are still new; the child will not use the extra features for two years. See the [10-year guide](/en/best-robot-for-a-10-year-old/).

## Mistakes parents make

Overestimating autonomy. At 8, installing software, pairing Bluetooth and updating firmware are still a parent's job. The child's job is the program.

Choosing on the age printed on the box. Cross-check with reviews that mention build quality, the clarity of the manual and after-sales support.

Ignoring storage. A compartment box for screws and small parts, kept with the robot, avoids the single lost screw that ends a project.

## How to know it works

After the build, the child should be able to drive the robot from mBlock, then change one block, for example a speed or a wait time, and see the behaviour change. That loop is the whole point. A first program is written out step by step in [your first mBot program](/en/first-mbot-program/), and the [distance-measuring activity](/en/mbot-activity-measure-distance/) is a good second session because the number on screen moves when a hand moves.

If Bluetooth never connects, that is a setup problem, not a sign the child "isn't ready". Work through [mBlock Bluetooth troubleshooting](/en/mblock-bluetooth-troubleshooting/) before blaming the robot.

## Questions parents ask

### Is Scratch the same as mBlock?

Same idea, same gesture: coloured blocks that snap together. mBlock adds the categories needed to drive a robot and lets you upload the program to the board. A project file does not always move unchanged between the two, but a child who knows one is at home in the other within minutes.

### Do we need a Windows PC?

No. mBlock 5 also runs on macOS and on tablets. Check Makeblock's current system requirements against the device you will actually use, and make sure that device has Bluetooth.

### Should we buy mBot2 straight away?

Only if the child already uses Scratch fluently and you know they will move to Python within a year or two. Otherwise mBot gives the same first two years of projects at a lower price, and [mBot vs mBot2](/en/mbot-vs-mbot2/) explains what you would be paying extra for.
