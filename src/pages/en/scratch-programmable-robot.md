**Your child does Scratch at school and you want a robot they can program the same way?** That is a sound idea: it is the most natural step from on-screen games to robotics. “Scratch compatible,” though, does not mean the same thing from one robot to the next. This guide explains the three families of block robots, then compares the models that are worth it in 2026.

## “Scratch compatible”: three very different cases

### 1. Real Scratch (scratch.mit.edu)

The official Scratch site offers **hardware extensions** for a few devices, including the **micro:bit** board and some older **LEGO Education** ranges (today found mostly second-hand). The advantage: the child finds exactly the editor they use in class. The limit: you install **Scratch Link** on the computer and you need Bluetooth, and the choice of robots stays small.

### 2. Software built on Scratch: mBlock and the others

**mBlock**, Makeblock’s software, is built on Scratch 3: the same colored blocks, the same logic, the same stage with its sprites, plus blocks that drive the robot’s motors and sensors. A child who knows Scratch finds their way in a few minutes. You can use it [online with no install](/en/mblock-online-no-install/) or [in the installable version](/en/install-mblock-5-windows/), and you can switch to **Python**. Our comparison [mBlock vs Scratch](/en/mblock-vs-scratch/) spells out the differences.

### 3. “Cousin” block editors: MakeCode and in-house apps

**Microsoft MakeCode** (used with the micro:bit) and many manufacturers’ apps also offer blocks that snap together, but the interface is not Scratch’s. That is workable: the logic (loops, conditions, variables) is the same, and you only need a short time to adapt.

**In short**: if your goal is continuity with school, prefer Scratch or mBlock. If you are aiming at electronics and projects, MakeCode teaches just as much.

## How to choose in 30 seconds

- **Ages 6–8, discovering Scratch** → Codey Rocky.
- **Ages 8–12, likes building, wants ready-made projects** → mBot.
- **Age 10 and up, aiming at Python** → mBot2 (see also our [guide to a programmable robot in Python](/robot-programmable-python/)).
- **Prefers electronics and gadgets to rolling robots** → a CyberPi board or a micro:bit.
- **For a class** → see our [guide to a teaching robot for school](/en/robots-for-school/).

## Traps to avoid

1. **Trusting the words “Scratch compatible” on the box**: check whether that means official Scratch, software based on Scratch (such as mBlock), or an in-house app.
2. **Ignoring the device**: some robots are easier to drive from a computer than from a tablet. Scratch Link, for its part, needs a computer with Bluetooth.
3. **Buying something too simple for a child who is already comfortable in Scratch**: if they make games with variables and clones in class, an arrow-button robot will bore them. Start with mBot or mBot2.
4. **Forgetting the batteries**: the classic mBot and the micro:bit run on cells. Codey Rocky and the mBot2 have a rechargeable battery.
