At 10, often in CM2 (the last year of French elementary school) or sixième (the first year of collège, which is middle school, about ages 11–15), Scratch starts to feel short. The child can stay an hour, sometimes two, on the same project. They have already met loops, conditions, and variables. Python, the language where you write lines and the indent at the start of a line matters, becomes readable if someone is beside them the first few times. The useful gift is a robot that keeps blocks for the start and opens that text afterward, with enough sensors that next year does not repeat this one. This guide compares three paths: the mBot2, the CyberPi board on its own, and an Arduino car for children who are already at ease.

## Why ten is a turning point

A child who has Scratch recognizes a loop and a variable. They can read a short Python program without the page closing on them, and they understand that an indent at the start of a line changes the meaning of the text. This is the moment for a robot that does both, so the change is not a wall. A blocks-only robot becomes limited quickly. A text-only robot, with no visual step, discourages them at the first error message.

A session often runs from half an hour to an hour and a half. That is enough for a project over several days, for finding out why a sensor returns zero, and for explaining the program to someone. At 8, that length is often too long: the [age 8 guide](/en/robot-gift-for-an-8-year-old/) stays on Scratch. Around 12, some will want the Arduino language, which is close to C++. Ten leaves Python in its place.

School changes at the same time. In CM2, a robot can support science or technology: a measurement, a course, a write-up. In sixième, showing that you wrote Python counts in the schoolyard as much as the robot itself. The child also wants to connect the object to the world: home Wi-Fi, the phone, a real temperature or distance, an alarm, watering, a timer. These connected projects are often called IoT, for Internet of Things. Scratch on its own still feels like a game. Python and sensors give a project you can tell someone about.

## What to look at

The robot should open Scratch and Python. You start with the blocks the child knows, then you rewrite the same behavior in text. Seeing the two forms side by side makes the change clear. It is not a magic button on every program: part of it gets rewritten. The point is that the hardware stays the same.

A screen on the board changes how you use it. You display a value without leaving the computer on, you see at once why the program stopped, and you can carry a clock or a measurement display. A robot with no visual feedback forces you to guess.

Wi-Fi is for driving from the phone, reading something online, or sending a measurement elsewhere. Bluetooth links the tablet or the computer without a cable. At 10, that connectivity is what opens next year's projects. It needs the home router password, and sometimes a setting with an adult.

On sensors, you get the ultrasonic sensor, the line on the floor, and light again, as at 8. A gyroscope and an accelerometer (the robot's orientation), a microphone, a speaker, and ports for a sensor that is not in the box are useful additions. The more inputs the board accepts, the longer the projects last. An extra sensor is paid separately. Its price is not in the reading below.

Robot tutorials in Python, in French, are rarer than Arduino ones. Before you buy, find out whether the brand documents this model. Makeblock does, for the mBot2 and the CyberPi. ELEGOO documents Arduino above all. A robot already used in middle school also has teacher sheets.

Python is written on a computer. A tablet is a poor fit once you leave the blocks. This site's [Python path](/en/python-course/) assumes a computer and Python 3.

## What makes the gift fail

Jumping straight from Scratch to the Arduino language skips the step where the text stays readable. Python is the in-between step. The mBot2 is built for that. The ELEGOO car comes after, unless the child already writes Python and an adult knows the syntax.

At 10, a robot without Wi-Fi or Bluetooth limits ideas quickly: driving from the phone, showing a measurement that comes from somewhere else, keeping a trace of the sensors. That is not an add-on gadget. It is the criterion that separates this age band from the classic mBot.

Check the documentation before you pay. For the mBot2 and the CyberPi, this site and the Makeblock community provide guides in French. For ELEGOO, the kit's examples are about Arduino, not Python. A robot bought for Python and delivered with examples in another language ends up in the cupboard.

The hardware has to be able to follow. At 10, a program is short. Two years later, the projects get longer, and one more sensor extends the same robot. When Arduino or a Raspberry Pi becomes the subject, the [gift for a teen](/en/programming-gifts-for-teens/) takes over.

Finally, three sensors are enough in Scratch. In Python, the child wants to try sound, orientation, light, then a sensor that was not in the box. A robot with few inputs becomes repetitive as soon as the language itself moves forward.

## The first months

The first month is for moving from blocks to text, in six to eight sessions. First you redo a known program in blocks. Then you open the same behavior in Python, to see what happened to the loop and the condition. Toward the end of the month, a small program is written directly in text. You have understood it when the child can say what an indent or a repeat corresponds to, without copying at random. The [beginner Python path](/en/python-course/) covers the language. The robot itself is programmed in mBlock, or in the Arduino IDE for the ELEGOO car.

The next two months can aim at the network. Show on the screen a measurement the board already knows (light, sound, orientation). Drive a movement from the phone, if the maker's app allows it. Later, read a weather figure online or send a measurement to a spreadsheet. This guide does not describe those connections. They are directions, not a tutorial carried out here. [Intermediate Python](/en/python-intermediate-course/) works on the language you use to write them.

After that, a project can last several days. A robot that follows a line and avoids an obstacle is realistic with the mBot2 or the ELEGOO car. A GPS is not part of these kits. An alarm that reacts to movement, a sound played from the speaker, or a page opened in the phone's browser are follow-ons, not the program for December 25. The sign that it holds: the child can show the project a week later and explain a line.

### Before December 25

1. Read the delivery date on the listing. Aiming for before December 20 leaves a margin. After that, only the delay shown counts.
2. Install mBlock before the 24th, with the [first steps with mBlock 5](/en/first-steps-mblock-5/). For the ELEGOO car, that is the Arduino IDE, on a computer.
3. Charge the battery that came with it, or the batteries the manual names. A second set, a book, or an extra sensor: check the price. It is not in the reading.
4. Block two to three hours with an adult for the first Python session, and keep the Wi-Fi password within reach.

These kits stay at low voltage, on batteries or USB. You do not touch mains power. The risk on the first evening is missing software or an empty battery, not a dangerous connection.
