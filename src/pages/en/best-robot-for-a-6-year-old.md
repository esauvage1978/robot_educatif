**At 6, buy a robot the child can start without reading a manual.** In England that is often Year 1; in the US, 1st grade. The real split is whether you want arrows and no screen, or Scratch-style blocks on a tablet with you beside them.

Six is the bridge. At 5, two or three commands with an adult are enough. Around 7 or 8, blocks and simple sensors become realistic. At 6 the child can hold a longer path, notice a wrong turn, and stay with a game for about ten to fifteen minutes. They still need you for batteries, the first connection, and anything that looks like a construction set.

## What to look for

Already built, or a maze that snaps together in a few minutes. A 15-minute screwdriver build can be a weekend activity with you. It is a poor Christmas-morning toy if you wanted them to play before breakfast.

Skip Python, Arduino wiring and long parts lists. Those belong in the [10-year guide](/en/best-robot-for-a-10-year-old/) and the [12-year guide](/en/best-robot-for-a-12-year-old/).

On the listing, check three things: batteries in the box or not, whether a tablet is required, and the maker's age.

## A simple decision

No coding yet, no screen: Botley. They like tablets and you want one robot for several years: Codey Rocky. Several children and a lighter toy: the mouse. If they are nearly 7 and read short instructions, also read the [7-year guide](/en/best-robot-for-a-7-year-old/): an mBot, built with you in about 15 to 20 minutes, becomes reasonable.

## Questions parents ask

### Does a 6-year-old need a tablet?

No. Botley and the Code & Go mouse work with buttons. A tablet only matters if you choose Codey Rocky.

### Will they still use it next year?

An arrow robot usually offers one or two more years of new paths. A block robot such as Codey Rocky can continue if you accept the screen.
